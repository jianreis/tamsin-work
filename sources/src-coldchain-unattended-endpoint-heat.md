## Summary

Well-sourced reporting on the part of the chain that nobody monitors: what happens
to the medicine after the courier lets go of it. Even where a pharmacy has
validated packaging and a controlled route, the parcel can sit in a closed mailbox
or on a doorstep in direct sun for hours until the patient gets home. The article
frames this as the unresolved gap in mail-order pharmacy oversight in the United
States, with pressure building for temperature tracking that extends to the point
of patient possession.

This is the single most transferable finding for an unattended locker rail. A
locker is exactly the "dark box in the sun for an unknown number of hours"
scenario, with the added variable that the dwell time is set by the customer, not
by the operator.

## Key claims

- Even where pharmacies control transit conditions, a package may be placed in a
  dark mailbox or left on a doorstep in the sun for hours before the recipient
  collects it.
- Post-delivery, pre-collection dwell is effectively unmonitored in current
  mail-order practice.
- Extreme temperatures degrade medicines through several mechanisms, including
  evaporation of liquids, pressure change in aerosols and alteration of other
  components.
- USP guidance cited in the article: routine storage 68-77°F, with brief excursions
  to 59°F or 89°F tolerated.

## Verbatim excerpts

> "Even if pharmacies take all the steps to keep the medications within the proper
> temperature ranges during transit, a package might be slipped into a dark mailbox
> or dropped off at someone's door and sit in the hot sun for hours before the
> recipient gets home from work."

> "Extreme temperatures can degrade the effectiveness of medications, whether it is
> through evaporation of liquid, changing the pressure of aerosols or alteration of
> other components of drugs."
