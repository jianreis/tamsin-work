# Medication Collection & Delivery Journey — IDIs (pre-packed redemption)

## Summary

Primary qualitative research on **how customers redeem pre-packed
prescription medication** — in-store collection versus home/workplace
delivery. **15 remote 1-hour IDIs** (8 collection, 7 delivery), fielded
**15–19 December 2025**, participants who had redeemed pre-packed medication
at least twice in the prior 120 days; balanced across gender, race and
province (GP 5, WC 3, KZN 3, MP 2, EC 1, NW 1); ages 25–60. Scope: script
submission → notification → planning → arrival/waiting → handover →
counselling → payment/confirmation → exit. The report maps a six-stage
collection journey and a five-stage delivery journey, each with behavioural
patterns, worked/friction, and recommendations — plus a **mental-models
chapter** that is the study's distinctive contribution.

**Raw transcripts are not available** (confirmed by Tamsin, 2026-08-21) —
the participant quotes below are as the report prints them, attributed only
as "Collection participant" / "Delivery participant".

**Reliability — B (qualitative, small-n, justify):** company primary
research, n=15, purposive; quotes are individual accounts; the X/15 splits
describe the sample.

## The mental-models chapter (the headline)

Customers do not experience prescription fulfilment as retail — they
experience it as a **high-stakes health responsibility**:

1. **Medication is a high-risk responsibility.** Errors have real
   consequences, so customers default to familiar, proven routines and
   "optimise for predictability over innovation". "Customers are not
   resisting digital or new services, they are protecting themselves from
   risk."
2. **Medication is not online shopping.** No browsing or experimenting; the
   want is the same outcome every time. "Digital tools must reassure first,
   optimise second."
3. **Responsibility changes behaviour.** Managing for others amplifies risk
   sensitivity; customers "build safeguards into the journey" — familiar
   branches, known staff, manual follow-ups — and "would rather wait longer,
   travel further and use 'older' channels" for confidence. "Design must
   support customers as care coordinators, not just end users."

Load-bearing lines for the friction framing: manual follow-ups and routines
"act as **safeguards against uncertainty in a system they don't fully
control**"; "**digital submission shifts effort, not responsibility**" — the
customer still owns making sure the pharmacy actually has and processed the
script. (Direct bearing on the #09 friction panel's theme-3 framing
question — see Used in.)

## How the two channels are chosen

- **Collection = control.** Chosen by people managing layered medication for
  others (5/8 collect on behalf of children, parents, dependents): physical
  hand-in, choosing when to collect, a familiar branch. Not convenience —
  "maintaining control across multiple prescriptions, timelines, and people."
- **Delivery = continuity.** Chosen mostly for one's own stable chronic
  repeats (4/7); a one-time setup that becomes "part of a background system
  that 'just works'". Notably self-selecting: delivery users are people whose
  work-from-home flexibility or household setup means **someone can wait at
  home** — the channel excludes those who can't.
- **Urgency overrides channel:** urgent/once-off medication goes in-store;
  chronic/repeat suits pre-packed collection or delivery.
- **Reliability beats speed and digital sophistication**, across both
  cohorts. Digital comfort in daily life does not transfer to medication
  ("This is medication — it's not the same as ordering food"). The app plays
  a limited, supporting role; loyalty is habitual and branch-anchored, not
  rewards-driven ("I didn't even know about the new rewards").

## Key frictions (collection journey)

- **No single confirmation loop at submission.** Emailed/WhatsApped scripts
  give no proof of processing ("I sent it on WhatsApp, but I still wait to
  see if they've actually loaded it"); scripts get lost and re-sent, with
  patients acting as "proof-holders between systems"; doctor, PA, pharmacy,
  call centre and patient share no confirmation mechanism.
- **Notifications confirm readiness, not timing — inconsistently.** SMSes
  are missed, delayed or ignored; status between received/packed/ready is
  unclear; customers compensate with manual follow-ups or just going in
  ("I don't always wait for the SMS — I just go"), and buffer-collect days
  early.
- **Delays undermine the pre-packed promise.** System issues or staff
  availability erase the time saving; stress spikes when collecting for
  others, especially sick children.
- **Counter handover is smooth until it isn't.** Verification is routine and
  familiar; variability across branches and not-immediately-found medication
  shake trust.
- **Payment queues break the momentum.** Fast collection, then front-of-store
  tills behind full-trolley shoppers ("Now you've got people that have done
  their monthly shopping… and you've got to pay for your meds"); no option
  to pay at the pharmacy. Recommendation repeats earlier research: point of
  sale at the pharmacy, or a medication-only till.
- **Counselling: no frictions found** — labels are the trusted primary
  source; counselling is wanted for new/changed/high-risk medication only.
  Consistent with the Oct-2025 IDIs.

## Key frictions (delivery journey)

- **Setup is the high-trust moment.** Physical drop-off is a deliberate
  risk-mitigation strategy — digital setup "lacks tangible confirmation that
  the script has been correctly loaded for delivery".
- **Status states blur.** "Processing", "on the way" and "delivering" run
  together; customers adapt expectations downward rather than demand
  clarity.
- **Broad windows limit the day.** Waiting is passive and tolerated (these
  are customers who can be home) but "not knowing timing adds low-level
  tension" and the experience "varies by store".
- **Handover: no frictions found.** Quick, name-only verification; sealed,
  discreet packaging is a trust and privacy signal ("You can't see that it's
  medication from the outside").
- **No support channel after handover.** Accuracy is assumed rather than
  checked ("I haven't properly looked at it… we're so used to it"); if a
  question arises there is no built-in way to ask — recommendation is a
  shared post-handover support layer (WhatsApp/app pharmacist follow-up,
  call-back) for both channels.
