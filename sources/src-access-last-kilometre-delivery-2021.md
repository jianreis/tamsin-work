## Summary

Stated demand, measured on the people who would use it. Adams and colleagues surveyed 116 chronic patients queuing in the pharmacy waiting areas of four Cape Town public sector primary health care facilities over four weeks, asking whether they wanted their medicine brought closer and on what terms.

The appetite is high and it is willing to pay. 80.2% were interested in a medicine delivery service, 84.5% would order through a mobile application, and 77.6% would pay for the service. That last figure is the interesting one for a commercial concept: these are public sector patients receiving medicine free of charge, and three quarters would still pay to avoid the trip. It prices the collection burden without needing a cost-of-travel study.

One result cuts against a locker-first design. 96.8% preferred delivery to their home rather than to an alternative pickup location. A concept built on partner collection points has to reckon with that: the stated preference in this sample is for the door, and collection points win on cost and on addressing reality rather than on preference. Whether the preference holds where home delivery is unreliable or where the address is informal is not tested here, and Cape Town is not a rural sample.

## Key claims

- Sample: 116 chronic patients, four public sector primary health care facilities in Cape Town, four-week exploratory non-randomised quantitative survey, structured questionnaire administered in pharmacy waiting areas.
- 80.2% were interested in a medicine delivery service.
- 84.5% were willing to use a mobile application to order their medicines.
- 96.8% preferred that their medicines be delivered to their home.
- 77.6% were willing to pay for the delivery service.
- Patient demographics aligned with preferences on mobile ordering, fee for service and delivery location.
- Setting is metropolitan Cape Town public sector, so results do not generalise to rural or deep-rural patients.
