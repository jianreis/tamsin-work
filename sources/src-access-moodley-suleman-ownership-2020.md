## Summary

Moodley and Suleman geocoded the entire SAPC pharmacy register from 1994 to 2014 in Q-GIS and mapped every registration to province and district, to test whether the April 2003 deregulation of pharmacy ownership (which let non-pharmacists own pharmacies, and which enabled the corporate chain model Dis-Chem and Clicks now run) actually widened access.

It did not. Of 2,019 pharmacies that opened after the regulation, 52% landed in Gauteng, Western Cape and KwaZulu-Natal, the three provinces holding the major metros. National pharmacy density rose from 3.55 to 5.43 per 100,000 population, a gain of 1.88 per 100,000, against roughly 20.66% population growth. The chains added stores where the paying customers already were.

This is the causal counterpart to [[src-access-ward-pharmacy-equity-2014]]. Ward measures the gap; Moodley and Suleman show that the market mechanism most likely to close it has already been tried for two decades and did not. That is the strongest single argument in the evidence base for a non-store access node.

## Key claims

- Regulations opening pharmacy ownership beyond pharmacists came into effect on 25 April 2003, framed as improving access to pharmaceutical services.
- Method: SAPC pharmacy register 1994 to 2014, each registration assigned GPS coordinates in Q-GIS and mapped per province at district level.
- 2,019 pharmacies opened after the 2003 regulation.
- Of those new pharmacies, 52% opened in Gauteng, Western Cape and KwaZulu-Natal, the three provinces containing the major metropolitan areas.
- National pharmacy density rose from 3.55 to 5.43 pharmacies per 100,000 population over the study period, a net gain of 1.88 per 100,000.
- Population grew approximately 20.66% over the same period.
- Conclusion: opening ownership to laypersons increased the number of pharmacies but did not produce a substantial increase in access for previously disadvantaged and rural areas.
