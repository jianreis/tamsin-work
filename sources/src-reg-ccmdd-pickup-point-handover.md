# What CCMDD actually does at the moment of handover, and on what authority

## Summary

CCMDD is the reason anyone in South Africa believes off-site collection of
chronic medicine is possible, so it is worth being precise about what it proves
and what it does not. The programme dispenses centrally at a licensed service
provider pharmacy, pre-packs and labels a patient-named parcel, and moves it to a
pick-up point the patient chose. The point may be a private pharmacy, a
workplace, a church hall, a school or a post office. At the handover the patient
presents an identity document and a Medicine Collection Card; a proxy presents
the patient's card, the patient's ID and their own. In adherence clubs the
parcels are handed to a **lay club facilitator or community health worker** by
the pharmacist, and that lay worker distributes them to 25 to 30 patients at a
community venue.

So the public sector already hands dispensed scheduled medicine to patients
through people who are not pharmacists and not pharmacy support personnel, at
premises that are not pharmacies, at national scale. That is the strongest
factual precedent available for the concept.

The legal authority for it is the part that does not resolve. Nothing retrieved
in this session identifies a statutory exemption, a gazetted authorisation or a
SAPC ruling that makes lay handover lawful. The programme runs on NDoH policy,
SOPs and the National Adherence Guidelines. The implicit legal theory is that
dispensing was completed at the licensed pharmacy and the pick-up point performs
distribution rather than dispensing or sale, and that no sale occurs at all
because the State issues the medicine free. Both limbs of that theory are weaker
in a private retail setting, where a sale plainly does occur and where there is
no programme policy standing behind the arrangement. Academic evaluation of
adherence clubs itself calls for policies to regulate the role of lay healthcare
workers, which is an admission that the current position rests on practice rather
than on settled law.

## Key claims

- Medicine is **dispensed and pre-packed centrally** at a service provider
  pharmacy and distributed to the pick-up point the patient selected.
- External pick-up points include **private community pharmacies, general
  practitioners, workplaces, schools, church halls and community-operated
  adherence clubs**. Not all of them are pharmacies.
- At a **pharmacy** pick-up point, the pharmacist verifies identity and hands
  over the sealed parcel. This is the described norm at retail PUPs such as
  Clicks and Dis-Chem.
- Patients must present an **identity document and a CCMDD Medicine Collection
  Card**. A proxy must present the **patient's collection card, the patient's ID
  and their own ID**.
- In **adherence clubs**, groups of 25 to 30 stable patients meet roughly every
  two months at a facility or community venue; **the pharmacist provides all the
  pre-packed ART to the community health worker for distribution**, and a **lay
  club facilitator distributes the parcels** and does a brief clinical check.
- Parcels are collected within a defined window (reported as 5 business days) and
  uncollected parcels are returned per SOP.
- No statutory exemption, gazette notice or SAPC determination authorising
  non-pharmacist handover was located. Peer-reviewed evaluation recommends
  "further policies … to regulate the role of lay healthcare workers", which
  indicates the gap is real and acknowledged.

## Verbatim excerpts

> "CCMDD provides clinically stable patients seeking care in the public sector the
> opportunity to collect ART and/or other chronic medications at a certified
> community pick-up point of their choice."

> "On the day of the club, the pharmacist provides all the pre-packed ART for the
> club to the community health worker for distribution to club patients."

> "Group meetings [are] facilitated by a lay club facilitator who provides a quick
> clinical assessment and dispenses pre-packed ART."

> "Patients are required to take their identification document and their CCMDD
> Collection Card with them to collect their medication. [A person collecting on
> behalf of a patient] must take the patient's medicine collection card as well as
> the patient's ID and their own ID when collecting."

> "Further policies are recommended to regulate the role of lay healthcare
> workers, ensure the reliability of the drug supply and the funding of club
> activities."
