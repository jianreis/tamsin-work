# Summary

Transcript of the second OTC workshop (10 Sep 2026, 1h26m): the internal team
with the people running the two stores - Mohamed Fakir (Firaz, Melrose Arch
pharmacy manager), Arna Liebel (Irene pharmacy manager), and hub advisors
Terence Vukeya (Irene) and Monalisa Anyaoku (Melrose, attribution inferred).
The frontline evidence complicates the session-1 narrative in useful ways:
both managers defend decoupling for scripts, the friction decays as customers
learn the process, store sales mixes drift toward scripts as stores mature,
and the first hard revenue comparison surfaced (Melrose OTC turnover R50-100k/
month below a lower-LSM comparator store). Irene's solve is described
precisely for the first time - a dedicated back-of-house printer plus counter
handover, i.e. options A and B from the ladder fused, built with zero new
tech. Solution energy converged on the digital OTC planogram, which Firaz
frames as monetisable vendor space, not a cost.

**Reliability:** A as a record of what was said. Store figures quoted in it
(sales splits, turnover gaps, staffing ratios) are managers' own working
numbers, unverified against systems - B-tier until pulled from reporting
(Carol Mazaris has visit-ID reporting access).

# Key claims

## Melrose Arch (Mohamed Fakir - "Firaz")
- Open ~4 months. Early process friction has largely decayed: heavy daily
  "why is OTC like this" questioning at opening, "lately much less friction";
  return customers ticket without help. Friction follows a learning curve.
- Perception vs measurement: a customer claimed a 30-minute wait; the system
  showed 44 seconds of pick-and-pack plus 10 seconds signing. The journey
  FEELS longer because the customer moves A→B→C, not because it is longer.
- Trust mechanism named: "If I can't see you, I can't put the pressure on
  you" - customers believe visible pharmacists work faster; invisibility
  reads as laziness risk. (Corroborates the Jan 2026 study's
  visibility/fallback theme [[src-dc-store-future-interviews-2026]].)
- Majority of OTC complaints come from OTC-only customers (Monalisa: "a huge
  majority of the OTC customers don't like the journey"; her hub-side pain:
  explaining why an OTC ticket P006 collects before script ticket P005 -
  queue-jump perception cuts both ways).
- **Defends the unified journey**: same-customer-journey was "one of our
  defining models"; warns against OTC and script experiences diverging too
  far. Nuance: a 1-2 item repeat script is picked as fast or faster than OTC
  (OTC requires typing product names; repeats are selected), so blanket
  "OTC is quicker" is wrong at small basket sizes.
- **First real revenue signal**: benchmarked against two stores of similar
  size opened ~a month earlier - Santon Gate (similar LSM; similar OTC
  numbers) and Blueberry Square (lower LSM) - Blueberry does ~R50-100k/month
  MORE OTC turnover than Melrose, where higher LSM would normally mean
  higher OTC. He attributes the gap to the missing display ("marketing and
  display concern"), not the process. "6 out of every 10 patients who come
  to buy one item will see a second item on the shelf that they're
  interested in."
- Counter-signal: no visible prices makes premium upsell EASIER - he has
  sold more of an expensive antihistamine (~R394) than ever, because the
  cheaper anchor product is not sitting beside it.
- Upsell quality now depends entirely on the individual staff member's
  product knowledge and salesmanship - the display used to do that work.
- Two customer types confirmed from the floor: pop-in/pop-out ("I just want
  my meds") vs happy-to-shop; frequent dispensary visitors (4x/month) do not
  shop 4x/month; customers voice the cynicism "you just want us to spend
  more money in the store".
- Confirms Clint's hypothesis: scripts are a fulfilment process, OTC is
  advice-and-discovery led (ads, packaging, promotions create the
  inquisitiveness; scheduled medicine cannot be advertised, OTC can - with
  legal limits on what a pharmacy may display, to be checked).
- Compliance: every Schedule 1/2 sale must run through Unisolv (register of
  who/what/quantity); Clint's legacy experiences of Allergex/Otrivin handed
  over the counter for front-till payment were "incorrect process" - the
  no-OTC-standard finding confirmed from the professional side.
- **Staffing is his real agenda**: opened with 9 staff (double a conventional
  store of his R1.8m turnover), now 8, deliberately stress-testing at 5-6.
  Ratio rule: roughly one picker in back per dispenser in front; overweight
  the front and the queue just moves to pick-and-pack, "a greater friction
  point... because of the lack of trust". Small stores (some <R1m turnover,
  3 permanent staff) cannot dedicate roles - a dedicated OTC printer/picker
  "works magically" only with enough hands. His rollout question: how do
  smaller stores run multi-process designs at all?
- **Store mix drifts as stores mature**: Melrose opened at 72:28 OTC:
  dispensary, moved through ~65:35, now ~45:55 (55 dispensary). "Generally
  when you open, you're an OTC pharmacy mainly."
- His verdict: keep the process, fix the display; accepts that at peak, OTC
  through the shared flow is slower than a conventional store's OTC counter.

## Irene (Arna Liebel + Terence Vukeya)
- Why they changed: new patients with very long scripts (one had 24 items)
  made one-item customers wait 6-7 minutes; complaints = "queuing twice";
  ~80% of Irene customers do not want to shop - they hover, congesting the
  Hub and blocking aisles.
- **The workaround, precisely**: the dispenser keeps the OTC customer AT the
  counter; the OTC label prints to a dedicated back-of-house printer (#9);
  whoever is free in the back treats the print as a visual cue, picks
  immediately and brings the item to the dispenser, who hands it over.
  Staffing 5 front / 3 back (or 4/4); the "OTC person" is primed, not
  dedicated. Scripts with OTC add-ons still follow the normal collect flow -
  the fast path is OTC-only. Built with zero new tech: this is options A
  (priority routing) and B (counter delivery) from the ladder, fused.
- Results: "I honestly didn't have any complaints after we changed it" - and
  no fairness complaints from script customers, because the legacy two-queue
  mental model ("scripts always take longer") legitimises OTC moving faster.
- At peak: Arna insists the fast path HELPS (keeps the dashboard from
  flooding, clears the virtual queue, reduces Hub pile-up), softer than
  Terence's session-1 account; the residual Saturday chaos she attributes to
  understaffing (ran 6 when the store needs 8) and long new scripts, not to
  the OTC path. Weekend-store economics: operationally needs 8 (4/4), the
  figures don't justify 8 - mirror image of Firaz's staffing problem.
- Irene's mix last month ~60-70% OTC, expected to drift toward scripts as
  the repeat/new-script base builds ("we are actually increasing in
  prescriptions now").
- Retention framing for why they broke process: "we'd rather keep the
  patients we're getting than chase them away for waiting for one OTC item."
- Terence: customers who can't SEE the product often don't know its name -
  "I'll actually [go] see what I'm looking for" = walk-outs; education need
  is constant; ticket-then-no-callout within promised wait time drives
  complaints (staff responsiveness, not process).
- Careline evidence for the sick-person guardrail: a 24-point complaint from
  a woman collecting for her husband waiting in the car post-operation -
  told repeatedly to "go shop".
- Display anecdote that proves the digital trigger: two vendor products
  rotate on the back screens (currently CTU ad space - Rynex, Iliaden, ACC,
  Ibumol); customers see ACC and say "oh, I forgot - please add ACC".
- Decoupling win for scripts, from the floor: local doctors bringing 4-5
  scripts are "extremely impressed" - earlier scripts are picked in parallel
  while later ones are still being dispensed.
- Ops tweak already made: collections called from ANY counter (screens
  hard-pointing to counters 6-8 made people cluster there; Jaco fixing);
  dispense-first, collections-when-free calling discipline.
- Tech parity gap: Melrose lacks Irene's collection call-out flow - Monalisa:
  "Melrose is now the forgotten child." Store comparison is confounded by
  different digital assets, not just different processes.

## Solutions the frontline backs
- **Digital OTC planogram on the existing screens** - both stores endorse;
  Firaz: "the planogram... would make a massive difference"; the ACC
  anecdote shows it triggers recall even at n=2 products. Composition
  guidance: ~70:30 or 80:20 preferred ("red line") to non-preferred lines -
  red-lines-only loses sales; dead stock is only ~2-3% of holding, so the
  old messy-wall objection is smaller than assumed; **rotating slots can be
  SOLD to vendors** (the physical CTU/eye-level-shelf model, digitised) -
  the fix can be a revenue line, not a cost.
- **Interactive categorised browse screen** (pain & fever, sinusitis, ...)
  at the Hub or dispensary counter - occupies waiting customers, arms the
  weaker salesperson, aids the can't-remember-the-name customer. Check
  advertising legalities for pharmacy display of S1/2.
- Pre-ticket OTC basketing on tablets (session-1 idea) noted with the
  planograms as the digital input it would need.
- Bricks (Clint's question): single customer profile with linked dependants
  is expected to replace today's nested-profile mess (per Tanya Roos).
