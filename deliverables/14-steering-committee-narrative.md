# Steering committee - narrative structure for the scripting hour

A working skeleton for the ~60-minute scripting slot, built to be refined
before any slides exist. The audience is the CEO, MD and two Pharmacy
executives. Roughly 15-20 minutes belong to the ROWA and store-selection
piece, which leaves about 40 minutes for everything else, including the
problem statement, the cost-to-serve levers, the CX story and the close.

## The spine

One sentence the whole hour hangs off:

> **Scripting costs too much to serve and asks too much of the customer - and
> the same redesign fixes both.**

The hour is one story with two engines. Part one makes a script cheaper to
dispense. Part two makes the experience worth choosing Dis-Chem for. The hinge
between them is the pharmacist: automation buys back pharmacist hours, and the
CX work is what those hours get spent on - the clinical attention, the named
check, the person who stays reachable. Said once at the open, once at the
handover to the CX half, and once at the close, this is the line that stops
the session reading as two unrelated presentations.

This framing also does the renaming work without a rename: "future of
scripting" has stuck as a label, so rather than fighting it, the problem
statement defines it - the whole pharmacy experience, acute and repeat,
cost to serve and employee experience and customer experience together.

## The shape of the hour

| # | Beat | Who | Time | The one message |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Problem statement | Tamsin | 4 min | Scripting is expensive to serve and effortful to use; this project attacks both at once. |
| 1 | Cost to serve: the levers | Tamsin (or colleague) | 5 min | We identified 6-7 levers; here is where each one stands. |
| 2 | ROWA + store selection | Colleague | 15-20 min | The machine, the model that picks stores, the network fit, the first two stores. |
| 3 | The hinge | Tamsin | 1 min | The machine buys back pharmacist hours; the second half is what the customer gets for them. |
| 4 | What the world taught us | Tamsin | 5 min | Three themes from the global research, each with one exemplar and one number. |
| 5 | The strategic opportunity | Tamsin | 3 min | The thesis slide: where Dis-Chem wins and why now. |
| 6 | The repeat journey, as a story | Tamsin | 10-12 min | One household, told in moments, concepts introduced inside the story, ending on the WhatsApp walk-through. |
| 7 | Acute, in one slide | Tamsin | 2 min | The same capabilities carry the acute journey; detail in appendix. |
| 8 | The asks | Tamsin | 4 min | The specific decisions this committee is being asked to make. |
| 9 | Close | Tamsin | 1 min | Back to the spine sentence; the road ahead on one slide. |

That totals 50-57 minutes. If the ROWA piece runs to 20, the recoverable
minutes live in beats 4 and 7, never in beat 6 or beat 8 - the story and the
asks are the two things the room must leave with.

## Beat by beat

### 0. Problem statement (4 min)

Open on the customer and the cost in the same breath, with one hard number on
each side if the research supports it (queue-time or effort finding from the
as-is work on the customer side; cost-to-serve-a-script on the operations
side). Then the definition slide: what "future of scripting" actually covers.
Resist the temptation to recap the project history here - the room was at the
kick-off, and the levers section carries the reminder function anyway.

### 1. The levers, on a page (5 min)

One slide, all 6-7 levers, each with a status: live with early results, in
discovery or POC, or next. Then one slide per *live* lever showing outcomes -
numbers, however early, because this is the only part of the hour with
results rather than intent, and executives will anchor on it. The
discovery-stage levers (OCR, ROWA) get named here but not explained - ROWA is
about to get twenty minutes, and OCR gets one line on where discovery stands.
End on "what's next" as a single roadmap line, not a section.

### 2. ROWA and the store model (15-20 min, colleague)

His content, but two narrative requests worth agreeing with him beforehand.
First, that he opens by placing ROWA on the levers slide from beat 1, so his
piece reads as the deep-dive on one lever rather than a separate project.
Second, that he ends on the first two stores by name - because beat 8 can
then propose piloting the CX work in the same two stores, which turns the
two halves of the hour into one pilot decision.

### 3. The hinge (1 min, one slide)

The single connecting move, and worth a deliberate slide rather than a spoken
aside: the machine takes the toil, the pharmacist takes the customer. If the
first half of the hour was about the cost of a script, the second half is
about what the customer gets. This is where the employee-experience thread
surfaces too - the pharmacist freed from counting and fetching is the same
pharmacist the CX concepts keep visible and reachable.

### 4. What the world taught us (5 min)

Themes, not the eleven patterns - the pattern library is the appendix and the
leave-behind. Three themes carry the argument, each landed with one named
exemplar explained from scratch and one number pulled out as a callout:

1. **The paper dies, and a token replaces it.** End-to-end digitisation of
   the script itself, done the low-friction way. Australia's e-script is a QR
   code in an SMS: scan it at any pharmacy, no app, no PIN, forwardable to a
   family member. Germany tried the opposite - a PIN-gated app - and roughly
   99% of patients fell back to paper. The lesson for SA is that the fallback
   is the design: QR, WhatsApp, printed code, so it works in a loadshedding
   queue on a basic phone.
2. **The pharmacist moves up, the machine takes the toil.** The winning
   pharmacies abroad automate counting, packing and chasing so the
   pharmacist does clinical work only a pharmacist can do. This theme
   deliberately echoes the ROWA piece the room just heard - it tells them
   the machine is not just a cost lever, it is the global playbook.
3. **Experience is the retention engine.** Refills made automatic and
   visible are how the leaders keep chronic customers: synchronised
   collection dates, Amazon-style order tracking, refills that arrive
   without being asked for. The NHS App's tracking and CVS's
   default-on refill programmes are the exemplars; the number is the NHS
   App's repeat-order volume.

A fourth theme - the store estate as the fulfilment network, with Apollo in
India fulfilling online orders from its physical stores - is available if
time allows, and it flatters the network-optimisation work the room just
saw. If beat 2 runs long, it folds into theme 2 as a sentence.

### 5. The strategic opportunity (3 min)

One slide, stated as a claim the room can disagree with, not a vision
statement. The shape that follows from the research: Dis-Chem already owns
the assets the global winners had to build - the store network, the courier
service, the chronic-repeat base - and the opportunity is to connect them
with rails the customer already carries (WhatsApp on any phone) so that a
repeat script becomes something the household barely has to think about, and
an acute script becomes a same-visit, priced-upfront promise.

### 6. The repeat journey, told as one household (10-12 min)

The recommendation on structure, answering the open question directly: tell
one story, not two. Lead with the cash household on a basic phone (the Sipho
story), and use the insured persona as contrast beats inside it ("for an
insured customer, the same moment looks like...") rather than as a second
run-through. Two full persona walk-throughs in twelve minutes flattens both;
one lived story with contrast beats keeps the room in it.

Sequence inside the beat:

1. **The people** (2 min). The household in two or three sentences each -
   who they are, what a repeat costs them today in trips, queues and
   data. This is the only place persona material appears; the persona
   cards go to the appendix.
2. **The moments** (5 min). Walk the phases of the household-repeat journey
   using the moments structure already built in deliverable 09 - need,
   activity, moment - and introduce each concept *at the moment it earns its
   place*: Script Sync when the household lands on one collection date,
   CarerConsent when the daughter collects for her mother, the Standing
   Order at the order window, the Medicine Cabinet where the household sees
   itself. Concepts introduced inside the story need no separate concept
   parade; the 08 concept cards become the leave-behind.
3. **The proof on a basic phone** (4 min). The WhatsApp walk-through
   (deliverable 11), shown as the actual conversation, message by message -
   ideally the built mock on screen rather than screenshots on slides. The
   point to land explicitly: everything the room just heard works in a chat
   thread on the cheapest phone in the room, cash paid at a counter, no app
   download, no smartphone assumption. For this audience the walk-through is
   the moment the work stops being abstract.

### 7. Acute, in one slide (2 min)

Do not walk the acute journey - claim it. One slide showing that the same
capabilities carry it (the E-Script Token, price before travel, Express
Collect) at a different tempo, with the acute moments work (deliverable 10)
and the Thabo mock (deliverable 12) named as appendix material the committee
can request. If a pharmacy executive pulls on this thread, that is a good
sign, and the appendix is ready.

### 8. The asks (4 min)

A steering committee exists to decide things, so the hour must end on
decisions, stated as specific, answerable requests. Candidate asks to refine
with the leads before the session (choose two or three, not five):

- Endorse the CX direction - the repeat-first, WhatsApp-first shape - as the
  basis for detailed design.
- Agree that the first two ROWA stores double as the CX pilot sites, so the
  cost lever and the experience land as one measurable pilot.
- Sponsor the regulatory work the design has surfaced, in particular the
  legal read on acute delivery under the SAPC courier rule and the
  collection-point determination - these are on the project's punchlist and
  they gate the acute and locker propositions.
- Confirm the priority segment for the pilot (cash repeat household vs
  insured chronic) if the committee has a view.

### 9. Close (1 min)

The spine sentence again, then one slide of the road ahead with dates. No
summary of the presentation - the asks were the summary.

## What stays out (and where it goes)

| Cut from the hour | Lives in |
|---|---|
| The 11 global patterns, individually | Appendix / leave-behind (patterns deep-dive) |
| The 08 concept cards as a section | Leave-behind; concepts appear inside the story |
| The insured journey as a full second walk-through | Contrast beats inside beat 6; appendix |
| The acute moments journey and Thabo mock | One claim slide (beat 7); appendix |
| Persona set beyond the household | Appendix (13-persona-cards) |
| As-is research detail, friction catalogue | One number in the problem statement; appendix |
| The 13 future-state principles as a list | Referenced in one line at beat 5; appendix |

## Why this order (the case to make to the leads)

Three arguments for the buy-in conversations with the Head of Design, Head of
Domain and the Executive Head:

1. **Results before vision.** The levers-with-outcomes section runs before
   any future-state material, so the room hears delivery first and ambition
   second. That ordering buys the CX half a more generous hearing.
2. **One pilot, not two projects.** Tying the CX pilot to the two ROWA
   stores converts "here is a machine" plus "here is a journey" into a
   single decision with a single measurement story: cost to serve down and
   experience up, in the same stores, on the same dashboard. It is also the
   strongest available answer to "why are these two presentations in the
   same hour".
3. **The demo carries the risk.** The WhatsApp walk-through is the beat most
   likely to be remembered and most likely to go wrong. Rehearse it as
   theatre: decide in advance whether it is live, a click-through, or a
   video capture of the mock, and have the static fallback in the deck.

## Open items before slide-building

- Agree the two or three asks (beat 8) with the leads - everything else in
  the deck exists to earn them.
- Agree with the colleague the two narrative requests in beat 2 (ROWA framed
  as one lever's deep-dive; ending on the two named stores).
- Confirm the hard numbers for beat 0 and beat 1 (the as-is effort number,
  the cost-to-serve number, the early lever outcomes) - the skeleton assumes
  they exist and are shareable with this audience.
- Decide the demo format for the WhatsApp walk-through and build the
  fallback.
- Check punchlist item 10(k) (acute delivery legality) before beat 7's claim
  slide names delivery for acute scripts; if the legal read is pending, the
  slide says collection and the ask in beat 8 covers the read.
