Store of the Future · internal working session

The OTC Solve

OTC medicine was folded into the script process: submit at one counter, wait, collect at another. Scripts earn that wait. OTC does not. Irene proved it within a week. Today we shape the options we take back to business.

Internal team only Pre-seeded options, not blank-page ideation ~90 min
Part 1

The problem, played back simply

15 min

The issue in one paragraph

In SOTF stores, all medicine sits behind the wall. OTC follows the same decoupled process as scripts: the customer submits at the drop-off counter, the order goes through pick and pack, and the customer collects from the collections counter, in the same queue as scripts. In every other Dis-Chem, OTC is advise, reach, hand over: stock sits behind the counter within the pharmacist's reach, and the customer walks away with it in under a minute.

What the original decisions were actually solving

Any option we pick must be scored against these intents, because they are real problems and they came from our own principles work.

Stock behind the wall "Never turn your back on the customer"

Kills the legacy pattern where a pharmacist abandons the customer at the counter for up to 5 minutes to hunt stock. Paired with "proximity without exposure": the work happens, but out of sight.

Decoupling "Fulfillment like a production line"

Turns dead queue time into roaming time, consolidates pick and pack, and keeps the counter for care, not fetching. For scripts, this demonstrably works.

One flow for OTC + scripts Space and resourcing

The SteerCo rationale: one counter, one process, less operational complexity, less floor given to the pharmacy.

The assumption that failed: decoupling creates value only when there is preparation worth waiting for. A script takes minutes to dispense, so the wait converts into roaming. OTC takes seconds, so the process adds a queue and a walk where the customer's benchmark, set in every other Dis-Chem, is immediate.

Who feels it

  • CustomerCan't get OTC immediately; must join a second queue, behind scripts, for medicine they used to be handed on the spot. The shared queue also creates the perception that their small item will take as long as a full script.
  • EmployeeNo reachable or visible stock. When a customer can't remember which product they bought, or the pharmacist wants to show two options side by side, there is nothing to point at or hand over.
  • BusinessFears falling OTC sales: if the customer never sees the product, nothing triggers or reminds the purchase. OTC is high-frequency, high-margin volume.

The two-store natural experiment

Melrose Arch runs the unified flow without strain. Its traffic is script-led and scheme-heavy, the mission the process was designed around. Irene broke the process within a week. Its traffic is more OTC-led, advice-led and cash-paying. Staff built two workarounds: the pharmacist walks to the back and fetches (which reinstates exactly the turn-your-back behaviour the design bans), or a picker prioritises the OTC line and runs it to the pharmacist's counter for handover.

Why business should care beyond one store: the Dis-Chem estate looks far more like Irene than like Melrose Arch. If the unified flow only holds in high-LSM, script-dominant stores, Irene is not the exception. It is the preview of the network, and this needs solving before concept-to-scale.

One reframe worth saying out loud: the picker-runs-it-forward workaround is not vandalism, it is a prototype. Staff kept the pharmacist facing the customer, kept stock behind the wall, and collapsed the second queue. Frontline workarounds are unpriced user research.

Part 1b

What evidence do we actually have?

10 min

We have (weak)

  • Anecdotal staff reports of customer frustration, second-hand, not primary source.
  • The Irene workaround itself: observed behaviour, one week in, converging on one pattern. Treat it as evidence, not noise.
  • No meaningful process data from Irene, because the process being measured is no longer the process being run.

We have (indirect)

  • Jan 2026 SOTF study (n=12): "waiting is fine, being unsure isn't"; predictability beats speed. Limit: for OTC the anchor is instant, so even a predictable wait is a downgrade.
  • No OTC time-and-motion exists anywhere in our research base, for SOTF or legacy stores.

We can get, cheaply

  • Unisolv mission mix per store: OTC-only vs script vs script+OTC transactions. The number that proves the store-archetype argument.
  • Qmatic timestamps at Melrose (ticket → called → ready → collected) split by order type: what the clean process actually costs an OTC customer.
  • C4C complaints for both stores since opening.
  • One observer morning in a legacy store: a stopwatch and a tally sheet gives us the time-and-motion baseline. 40 observations beats zero.
  • Structured chats with Irene staff: what exactly they do, when, and why.
Part 1c

Guardrails for every option

5 min

Whatever we propose must preserve:

  • Never turn your back on the customer. The 5-minute abandonment must not come back.
  • The counter is for care, not admin (or fetching).
  • The script flow stays untouched. Decoupling works for scripts; we are fixing OTC only.
  • No return of the cage.
  • Cheap and reversible. Automated pickers (ROWA-class) dissolve this problem entirely by shooting any item to the front in seconds. This is a bridge for manual-picker stores, so no heavy capex the automated store makes redundant.
Part 2

Four options, a ladder of walk-backs

40 min

Each rung walks back one more of the original decisions. Gold dots = how much of the original design each option unwinds. We pressure-test each against the intents in Part 1, then decide what evidence would confirm or kill it.

A
The OTC express lane
walk-back
Fix the queue, keep everything else
Mechanism

A separate Qmatic ticket class for OTC with its own SLA (2-3 min) and picker prioritisation. OTC never queues behind scripts, and the collections screen shows the faster lane, which kills the "I'm stuck behind twenty scripts" perception.

Walks back

Only "same queue and SLA as scripts". Nothing physical changes.

Preserves

Every original intent, fully.

Trade-offs

Still two stops and a wait where legacy had none. Consumes picker capacity at peak. Does nothing for show-and-explain. Business will like this most; customers will like it least.

Evidence to validate

Qmatic timing at Melrose: can a 2-3 min SLA actually be met at peak with current picker headcount?

B
Counter delivery (the Irene prototype, formalised)
walk-back
One stop: the runner brings OTC to the pharmacist mid-conversation
Mechanism

OTC is picked on priority and delivered to the pharmacist's counter while the pharmacist stays with the customer: advise → runner delivers → hand over → done. One stop, no second queue, the pharmacist never turns their back, stock stays behind the wall. We would be instrumenting and SLA-ing what Irene staff already invented, not inventing something new.

Walks back

Decoupling, for OTC only: collection collapses into submission.

Preserves

Never-turn-your-back, proximity-without-exposure, production-line fulfilment, script flow.

Trade-offs

Counter dwell time rises, so the submission queue slows: it trades throughput for interaction quality. Needs a runner role and a dashboard priority lane, plus a hard rule stopping scripts leaking into the fast path.

Evidence to validate

Irene staff interviews (how it actually runs); measure counter dwell and queue length with vs without; runner capacity model.

C
The Pareto shelf
walk-back
A curated fast-mover range back within the pharmacist's reach
Mechanism

The top ~50-100 fast-mover OTC SKUs (likely most of OTC volume; Unisolv can confirm) return to within arm's reach: under-counter drawers, or a visible-but-not-grabbable display behind the counter. Everything else stays behind the wall. Reaching is not fetching: the 5-minute abandonment this rule targeted does not apply to an arm's length.

Walks back

"All stock behind the wall", partially.

Preserves

No cage, no fetching walk, script flow untouched. Restores show-and-hand for the SKUs that matter.

Trade-offs

Planogram and space in a fixed fit-out; inventory split across two locations (pick accuracy, stock counts); the shrinkage argument returns; visibly softens a flagship design statement. Expect this to be the contentious one.

Evidence to validate

Unisolv SKU-level OTC volume curve: what % of OTC transactions do the top 100 SKUs cover?

D
Split the flows
walk-back
A distinct OTC / advice service point, separate from script submission
Mechanism

A dedicated "pharmacy express" counter (it could live at the digital-wall end of the counter run) that serves OTC and advice as its own flow, effectively re-admitting that OTC and scripts are different products deserving different processes.

Walks back

The unified OTC + script decision itself.

Preserves

Decoupling for scripts, where it demonstrably works.

Trade-offs

Exactly the space-and-resourcing cost the unification decision was taken to avoid, plus wayfinding burden. The most direct challenge to a SteerCo decision, so it must be argued with volume data, not preference. Also the most honest fit for Irene-type stores.

Evidence to validate

Mission mix per store; peak-hour OTC arrival rates; staffing model for a second service point.

The overlay: show and explain, without stock

Attaches to A or B (and weakens the case for C). Solves the employee and business frictions digitally:

  • Pull forward the dispensary digital wall's "digital OTC shelves" — already a deferred SOTF work parcel (planned H2 2026). We are not proposing something new; we are pointing something already planned at this problem.
  • Counter tablet or product cards for "show me the options" moments: pack shots, sizes, prices, side by side.
  • Better Rewards purchase-history lookup for "I can't remember which one I bought": the pharmacist looks it up. Arguably better than squinting at a shelf, and it is an ecosystem selling point.
  • Display-only dummy packs (empty boxes): the visual purchase trigger business wants, at zero shrinkage risk.
Cross-cutting question for the room, not a fifth option: does the answer vary by store archetype? Melrose keeps the unified decoupled flow; Irene-type stores get B or D. Concept-to-scale already plans by store format, so a per-archetype answer is legitimate, not a fudge.

Part 3

Validation plan & how we decide

15 min

We present options back to business with trade-offs, not a single answer. Each option leaves this room with a named owner for the evidence that would confirm or kill it.

Data pull · owner ___Unisolv mission mix per store (OTC-only / script / script+OTC), plus the SKU-level OTC volume curve for the Pareto question.
Data pull · owner ___Qmatic timing extract at Melrose Arch by order type: ticket → called → ready → collected. What the clean process costs an OTC customer today.
Field · owner ___One observer morning in a legacy store: OTC service times, counter-abandonment counts, fetch durations. Our time-and-motion baseline.
Field · owner ___Irene staff interviews: walk the workaround step by step, when it triggers, who runs, what breaks.
Data pull · owner ___C4C complaints for both stores since opening, coded for OTC / queue mentions.
Session output · owner ___Options pack for business: problem playback, four options, trade-off table, per-option evidence status, recommended pilot (likely at Irene, since the behaviour is already live there).
