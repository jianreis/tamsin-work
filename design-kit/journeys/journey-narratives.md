# Journey narratives - the intent and the story, per persona

Companion pieces to the two moments journeys: short narratives that sum up what
each journey is for and what it feels like, one per persona. The pattern is the
Aisha narrative written during the #10 build (preserved below): **The Intent**
(what we're striving for), **The Story** (what we're building, walked as one
person), **The shape of it** (the journey's emotional arc in three lines).
Every scene in these narratives traces to an activity or moment in the parent
journey spec - [[design-kit/journeys/to-be-acute-moments]] (#10) and
[[design-kit/journeys/to-be-household-repeat-moments]] (#09) - which carry the
research citations. Concepts named in passing live in the
[concept set (#08)](../../deliverables/08-concepts.html).

The golden thread across all four intents (agreed with Tamsin, 2026-08-19):
today the system decides the treatment and then leaves the customer to make it
happen - the finding, funding, fetching, remembering all sit on them; the
future state is treatment orchestration, Dis-Chem partnering with the customer
by taking every one of those jobs onto itself and leaving only the decisions
that are genuinely the customer's. Each intent names the burden in that
journey's terms and each inversion names what Dis-Chem takes over.

Naming note: the #10 cash persona is **Thabo** (Sipho is #09's cash persona) -
the "#10 Sipho" request of 2026-08-18 is fulfilled here as the Thabo narrative.

Prose register: these are deliverable-adjacent storytelling pieces in the
register of the original Aisha narrative, kept deliberately; per
[[design-kit/templates/concept-card-template]] a deliverable's own presentation
needs supersede the general prose rules where they conflict.

## #10 · The acute script - Aisha (insured, her own illness)

*The original, written during the #10 build. Preserved as the pattern.*

### The Intent

This is what we're striving for.

You wake up ill. Not managing-a-condition ill - today ill. A UTI that flared
overnight; a child coughing at 2am. And here is the thing about being ill
today: you have exactly one goal, which is to start getting better, and
precisely zero capacity for anything else. Every form, every queue, every
"we'll call you back", every rand of uncertainty is effort you don't have. The
sicker you are, the truer this gets.

Today's journey doesn't care if you're not feeling well. You see a doctor, and
then the healthcare system hands you a slip of paper and quietly makes you the
project manager: carry the script, find a pharmacy, hope it's in stock, wonder
what it costs, queue while feverish, discover the co-pay at the till, get home,
realise you're not sure about the dose. The person with the least capacity does
all the coordinating.

The experience we're designing inverts that. The moment the doctor decides what
you need, the getting-better has already started - and everything between you
and the first dose is Dis-Chem's job, not yours.

### The Story (for Aisha)

This is what we're building.

You're still in the chair, and the deciding is done with you rather than after
you. The doctor, your record and the pharmacy are in the same conversation -
what's right for you, what it costs you (your scheme's answer, or the rand
number if you pay cash), what's actually on a shelf near you, generic or brand
chosen on medical advice, not at a counter. You walk out of the room with
nothing to organise. That's the first promise.

The second promise is that the system now visibly works for you while you go
lie down. The script is on your phone, and - because trust hasn't been earned
yet, this may be your first time - the service shows its work without demanding
anything: we have it, a named pharmacist has checked it, it's packed, it's on
its way, here's the driver on the map. Or for Thabo: the price answered on
WhatsApp before he leaves the clinic gate, the "ready" message before he walks
anywhere, the PIN that opens a locker after his shift, because he shouldn't
drag a sick child across town on hope. Nobody chases; nobody wonders; nobody
travels blind.

The third promise is that it ends properly. Medicine in hand isn't better yet -
the first dose today, instructions you can actually follow in your own
language, finishing all seven days. And if something breaks along the way -
stock, delivery, anything - the plan B arrives with the bad news, and a human
phones you. When you're ill, a dead end isn't an inconvenience; it's
abandonment.

And then - this is the quiet coda - the journey remembers you even though you'd
rather forget it. If the same thing keeps coming back, the pharmacy notices the
pattern the way a caring health provider would, and gently says so. A one-off
transaction ends by offering the beginning of a relationship.

### The shape of it

If #09 is a standing relationship - trust built over cycles, the service fading
into the background - #10 is a single pass, at your weakest. Dis-Chem gets one
shot, with a stranger who feels terrible, to prove that the moment care was
decided, the burden moved off their shoulders. The emotional arc runs from
depleted and braced for effort -> surprised the hard parts are already handled
-> better, and remembering who made it easy.

One line for the whole thing: you shouldn't have to be well to get your
medicine. Today's journey is designed for a healthy person to navigate; this
one is designed for a sick one.

## #10 · The acute script - Thabo (cash, his son's illness)

### The Intent

This is what we're striving for.

Your child wakes up ill. Not grumpy ill - coughing-through-the-night, quiet-in-
a-way-he-never-is ill. You have exactly one goal, which is to get medicine into
your boy - and here is the thing about a script in today's world: it isn't
medicine yet. It's a list of jobs. Find a pharmacy. Spend the taxi fare before
anyone will say whether the medicine is even on a shelf. Queue with a sick
child on your arm. And only then hear the price, said out loud at the till,
with the week's money counted in your pocket and a queue behind you - knowing,
because you've done it before, what walking away looks like.

The doctor's part took ten minutes. Every job after those ten minutes is yours
- the finding, the travelling, the hoping, the paying - carried at your own
risk, with your own money, for your own child. The treatment was decided by a
professional; making it happen was left to the parent with the least slack to
absorb anything going wrong.

The experience we're designing inverts that. The moment the doctor decides what
your son needs, the jobs move across the counter: Dis-Chem does the finding,
the pricing, the checking, the holding and the carrying - and your side shrinks
to the one thing only a father can do, which is be there. Every rand is named
before you spend one.

### The Story (for Thabo)

This is what we're building.

The clinic isn't on anyone's system, and it doesn't matter. The script is paper
in your jacket pocket, but while you're waiting to pay for the consult you
photograph it and send it to Dis-Chem on WhatsApp - one photo, almost no data -
and the job leaves your hands. Before you and your boy reach the clinic gate,
the reply: the script read back so you can see they have it right, the price in
rands, and a cheaper generic the doctor would approve, with the exact saving
shown. Nobody made you ask. You know the number - and that you can afford it -
before a single rand goes to a taxi. That's the first promise: the till never
gets to surprise you again.

The second promise is that the system now visibly works for you while you make
your way to it. The handover was your choice too - the express unit on the wall
for people in a hurry, or the collections counter, and you chose the
pharmacist, because it's his first antibiotic. Then the taxi, your son asleep
against your arm, and the thread moving while you ride: made up at your store,
each item scanned against the doctor's script, and then, in your language,
"Checked by Sindi - the dose is right for his weight." Someone with a name
stood behind your boy's medicine, and you watched it happen. The counter is
expecting you when you walk in; the pack has his name on it; the total is the
one you've known since the clinic. And on the days the shift can't spare you,
the store closing doesn't matter either: the express collect on the outside
wall is lit after dark - card, code, door, pack - and the duty pharmacist
phones you on the road home, because closing time shouldn't stand between a
working father and his son's medicine.

The third promise is that it ends properly. The first dose tonight, not
tomorrow - the syringe marked at his line, the labels and the guide in your
language, all seven days even when he seems better, written where you can't
miss it. On day three a question arrives, in your language: he should be
coughing less by now - is he? And when your answer is no, it doesn't bounce off
a chatbot. A pharmacist phones after six, listens, says it straight, and books
the clinic for tomorrow morning - with the price told before you say yes. Your
worry became a plan while you were making his supper.

And then - this is the quiet coda - nothing ever starts over. The new script
from the clinic flows into the same rails; everyone already knows the two of
you. By the weekend he's chasing his sister again, and the week's money did
exactly what you planned it to do.

### The shape of it

If Aisha's version of this journey is about effort - she's the patient, running
on empty - Thabo's is about risk. He isn't the one who's ill; he's the one
who's responsible, and today's journey makes him gamble on his son's behalf at
every step: the fare, the stock, the price, the closing time. This journey
takes the gambling away and leaves the caring. The emotional arc runs from
braced - money counted, queue expected, the walk-away rehearsed -> the number
known before anything was spent -> someone with a name behind his boy's
medicine -> better, with nothing wasted and nothing owed.

One line for the whole thing: no parent should have to travel on hope. Today's
journey prices the trip at the till, after everything has been spent; this one
prices it at the clinic gate, before a rand leaves his pocket.

## #09 · The household repeat - Nomvula (insured, the household medicine manager)

### The Intent

This is what we're striving for.

Chronic medicine has no finish line. Nobody is ever done with hypertension, or
diabetes, or a child's asthma - and in most homes it isn't one person's
medicine, it's everyone's: yours, your mother's, the kids'. Four conditions,
four cycles, four sets of dates, schemes and co-pays.

And here is the strange part: nothing about any of it ever changes - the same
medicines, the same doses, the same schemes - yet the work of getting it starts
from zero every month. Remember the dates. Redo the admin. Queue for an
identical bag. Learn the price again at the till, and prove all over again that
you may collect for your own mother. The system remembers nothing, so somebody
in the house has to remember everything - forever, on top of everything else,
and invisibly, because doing this well looks like nothing happening. Nobody
applauds when the medicine doesn't run out.

The experience we're designing inverts that. She tells the service everything
exactly once - who's in the household, how it reaches them, how it's paid, in
which languages - and from then on the remembering is Dis-Chem's: the dates,
the schemes, the stock, the renewals. Her side of the partnership is a set of
decisions made once; the running of them is theirs. The measure of success is
silence: the right medicine arriving on the same day each month, at a price
known at home, with nothing asked of her.

### The Story (for Nomvula)

This is what we're building.

One evening on the couch, and four people's medicine becomes one thing. You add
yourself, your mom, Zanele and Lethabo; your mother's consent is confirmed
once, not re-litigated at a counter; the scripts, the doctors and the cover are
already there, so nothing gets re-typed. Then the offer you'd never thought to
ask for: one date for everyone - and the plan is cleverer than a calendar,
because it knows Zanele's script can't move, so everyone else's shifts to meet
it, costs shown before you agree. The medicine calendar you've carried in your
head for years is gone. That's the first promise: asked everything once,
believed the first time.

The second promise is that it now runs by itself, where you can see it. A week
before run-out, one message: everyone's repeats are being prepared, arriving
Friday morning. The money is settled at home, not at a till - each scheme
charged first, your mom's shortfall visible in the same view, your card for
whatever's left, exactly as you set it up. The trip you no longer make still
does its double duty: the order stays open, so the winter vitamin C and
Lethabo's toothpaste ride along in the same parcel. Then the statuses you never
had to ask for: picked, checked by a pharmacist by name against everything all
four of you take, sealed, tracked to your door - where "delivered" means
scanned into your hands, not left at a gate. Inside, a month of medicine
already sorted into dose sachets, your mother's in her language. Most months
you do exactly nothing.

The third promise is that when something breaks - and something always breaks -
the bad news arrives with the plan attached. The scheme declines a line: the
message brings your choices with it. Stock runs out: the covered generic, or a
split delivery, your call in one tap. The driver can't reach you: the parcel
doesn't vanish into a depot, it waits where you tell it to. A script nears
expiry: the renewal started weeks ago, chased in the same thread - not by you,
phoning a receptionist between meetings. Whatever it is, it stays one
conversation, with a pharmacist one tap away, and you never start over.

And then - this is the quiet coda - the service earns the right to be ignored.
Trust here isn't one wow; it's twelve identical months in a row. The best month
is the one you can't remember anything about.

### The shape of it

If #10 is a single pass at your weakest, #09 is a standing relationship - a
service that proves itself cycle after cycle until it fades into the
household's background, noticed only if it breaks, which is why the breaking is
designed hardest of all. The emotional arc runs from overwhelmed and juggling
-> asked once, and believed -> each month quieter than the last -> the chore
simply gone, noticed only in its absence.

One line for the whole thing: nobody should have to run a small pharmacy to run
a household. Today's journey makes the busiest person in the house its unpaid
administrator; this one gives her the job back to Dis-Chem.

## #09 · The household repeat - Sipho (cash, collecting for himself and his mother)

### The Intent

This is what we're striving for.

The same condition, the same forever - and none of the same slack. Nothing
about Sipho's tablets changes from one month to the next, but every month he
has to earn them all over again: taxi fare to a store that isn't near anything,
a queue that costs a morning's work, a price learned at the till when the fare
is already spent - and then the whole exercise a second time, on a different
date at a different place, for his mother's clinic parcel. The medicine is
routine; getting it never gets to be. Two trips a month, forever, every step
his to plan and his to pay for, around money that arrives on one day.

Today's journey also quietly assumes things Sipho doesn't have: an app worth
the data, a street address, airtime to burn, slack in the week and slack in the
wallet. What he has is a cheap Android, WhatsApp, a payday, and a bank branch
by the taxi rank he already visits. The experience we're designing starts from
what he has, not from what the system wishes he had - and takes the rest onto
itself: the remembering, the pricing, the carrying, the checking. What stays
with Sipho is the one decision only he can make - when the money moves. It
never spends his money for him.

### The Story (for Sipho)

This is what we're building.

It starts at a counter you already use, and finishes on the phone you already
own. A WhatsApp after a collection, a question to the pharmacist on the next
visit, and you sign up in the chat, in your own time, in your language - no
app, no forms, no queue. Your mother's consent already covers you managing her
medicine, so two people's medicine becomes one thing in a few minutes. And when
the service offers to line your dates up, it takes no for an answer: the
programme's date lands in the week before payday, so you decline - and move
your own tablets instead, to the 28th, after payday, for a small once-off
top-up priced before you say yes. The chat keeps both dates so you don't have
to. That's the first promise: the service bends to the money, and the money
never bends to the service.

The second promise is that you never again travel on hope. The price comes in
rand, itemised, before you go anywhere - so you know the trip is affordable
before a cent goes to taxi fare. The message comes when the parcel is
physically in the locker at the Capitec by the rank - where you already go, no
street address needed, because you don't have one. You pay the exact amount you
were told at the bank counter, scan your code, and the locker opens; you check
the contents right there against the list in the chat, not at home after the
taxi. And behind all of it ran the same check the insured get: picked, scanned
against the script, signed off by a pharmacist by name, in a message in your
language. How you pay has no bearing on how carefully your medicine is handled
- and for the first time, you can see that.

The third promise is that failure never costs you a trip. The locker goes down:
you know before you leave home, with a new pickup point already arranged. A
tablet is out of stock: the cheaper generic you already prefer, or a split so
the rest is ready on payday regardless. Anything confusing, anything wrong: one
reply - call me - and a person phones you back, in your language, and makes the
plan with you, not at you. Your taxi fare is never spent on a closed door.

And then - this is the quiet coda - there's the part nobody announces: dignity.
Nothing at the bank counter says medicine, or cash patient, or clinic
programme; just a plain parcel waiting for its person. Your mother's free
parcel and your paid one are handled with the same care, her instructions in
her language, the day-by-day sheet on the fridge doing the remembering. Nobody
needs you standing over them - and nobody at any counter knows your family's
business.

### The shape of it

The same standing relationship as Nomvula's - but where her version buys back
time, Sipho's buys back certainty. Every feature that reads as convenience on
the insured side reads as risk removed on his: the price before the trip, the
parcel confirmed in the locker before he moves, the failure known before the
taxi. The emotional arc runs from two trips and a held breath every month ->
asked once, in his language -> each month a known number on a known day -> the
only surprises left are the ones the service catches first.

One line for the whole thing: for the insured, this journey deletes a chore;
for the cash majority, it deletes a gamble. The medicine simply waits - near
home, on his date, at a price he knew before he left.
